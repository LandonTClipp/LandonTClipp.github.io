One of my favorite life philosophies is something everyone has heard: it's better to ask for forgiveness than permission. I'm currently sitting outside by an alpine river in the San Juan mountains called Mineral Creek, which is situated downstream of two high alpine lakes called Ice Lake and Island Lake. I was forced to this spot not by choice, but because I had overstayed my welcome at my last campsite at the top of this place called Molas Pass. I knew I was over the 14 day limit, but I wasn't sure how strictly it was enforced. Apparently, very strictly. A Bureau of Land Management Police Officer knocked on my trailer door around noon, seemingly disappointed in me.

<!-- more -->

I stepped outside and greeted him. He asked me how long do I think I've been staying there. I answered honestly that I wasn't exactly sure, but if I look on my calendar on my phone I could give him an answer. He told me there was a report that I had been here for 16 days. I got my phone and told him honestly that I've been there for longer than 16 days. 

It's these types of situations that you shouldn't lie. Well, you should almost never lie in general, but especially when you're dealing with the police. They have information you don't. I told him I was from Chicago, I hated living there, and I come to the mountains to escape. He asked me if I was working in the nearby town of Silverton. I said no, just working remotely. He said that was a bit of a grey area, because there are rules against long-term residency on BLM land and that I'm in violation of the rules.

He also said what's working in my favor is that my camp is clean, I'm not working in town, and that I'm not from the area so he's going to cut me some slack. He let me off with just a warning. With a wink and a nod, he told me that technically speaking, and he's not going to tell me how to get around the law, but he only has jurisdiction on BLM land, and that the National Forest land just around the corner would be free for me to camp on for an additional 14 days. 

He welcomed me to the state, wrote down my ID, and told me to leave in 24 hours. So that's what I did, and now I find myself in the San Juan National Forest, whose rangers apparently don't know anything about me, and who don't know I'm prone to staying past 14 days. Well, some rules can only be broken for so long. I'm in the system now. If you're not an asshole, you're honest, and you don't give the police grief, they'll often just let you off easy.

That's a funny way to draw parallels with my life, specifically my career. I don't think of myself as particularly intelligent, I was never the largest brain in the room, and I don't view myself as particularly unique either. I grew up in the middle of nowhere Illinois. I wasn't smart enough to get into the front door of the University of Illinois, so I went in through the back door of a local community college that had an agreement with the U of I to shuttle some students in who had good grades.

I didn't really have good grades at the U of I either. I think my GPA started off at something like a 3.9 at the community college and it decreased every semester, resting somewhere at 3.3 or 3.4. While I definitely tried very hard, I wasn’t the academic superstar that many of my classmates were, and I struggled to understand a lot of higher level math concepts in Computer Engineering, especially with probability, statistics, and analog circuit design. I got many C's, one C-, but thankfully no F's.

Part of it was that I knew grades only mattered for the first job. I neglected my studies in favor of the extracurriculars I was doing, like working for the School of Earth, Society and the Environment doing large dataset processing of atmospheric satellite data (that's another story for another day). I also selfishly neglected my class groups, who relied on me to finish our group projects, to work on these extra curriculars. It's a dog-eat-dog world out there.

Before I graduated college, I got a job offer at this "high frequency trading" company called Jump Trading out of Chicago, IL. I got the job primarily because of my extra curriculars; I was involved in this student-run supercomputing team that competed in the Supercomputing Conference's Student Cluster Competition. Our primary sponsor, of course, was Jump Trading. I used those names I had learned to get a foot in the door. 

My oh my, how important I felt. Jump paid for me to come to Chicago, this far and distant foreign land 2 hours away from where I lived, interviewed me for the whole day, and thought I was good enough. So they gave me an offer, of which I accepted. 

Actually, it was the only job offer I got. I interviewed at a few other places, but I wasn't interested in any of them. This was literally my only option. The pay also was unbelievable for a 21 year old. I had never known that kind of money before.

And so there I spent the next 5 years of my life. The job turned out to not be what I expected. I realized I didn't like the people, I didn't like the work we did, and I especially did not like the culture of trading. It was a brutal, political, back-stabby kind of place. Being the naive young boy I was, I became kind of an asshole. I wanted to prove myself to those around me, but I didn't have the humility to learn, and I especially did not have the self-awareness that the genuinely horrible ways I was being treated were partly because I was an asshole, partly because other people were assholes.

Long story short, it wasn't a good fit. Unfortunately, at the same time I was looking to leave Jump, Jump was looking to leave me, and they beat me to the punch. That's how life goes sometimes.

Of course, being fired from a job is never a pleasant experience. Whereas I thought I had more time, I was suddenly and unceremoneously cut off from my entire job at the drop of a hat. _Fuck._

And that's where my literary device comes back into play. I was forced out by a BLM officer from where I was living (ahem I mean temporarily residing), who knew I was violating the rules, but yet still treated me with grace and didn't give me a hard time about it, even though he absolutely could have. Being forced out never feels great, but there's usually good on the other side.

If I were to take a guess at why people seem to trust me nowadays, it's because the experience of being forced out of my first job was so traumatic that it made me realize one important truth: it's better to be trusted than it is to be smart. Of course, being smart is great, but you won't get far in life without other people advocating for you. That's what people have consistently done for me over these last three years. I showed up, I demonstrated humility, I put in the work, and I followed my curiosity wherever it took me.

Where do I find myself today? Well, I joined an AI infrastructure company called Lambda where I put my HPC chops to the test and built, and in some cases [significantly advanced](https://docs.lambda.ai/public-cloud/guest-agent/) the company. I spent a couple years there, learned a lot about how cloud companies work, made a decent amount of money, and in the last 6 months I was bored and just decided I wanted to learn how "Containers as a Service" works. I wrote a [couple](2025-10-21-gpu-containers-as-a-service.md) of [articles](2025-11-19-gpu-passthrough-boot.md) on it as part of my job, presented my findings to the company, and realized nobody really cared. Well, some people cared, but my research didn't fit the needs of the company at the time, so I took my knowledge to CoreWeave.

Joining CoreWeave was one of the weirdest experiences of my life. Not because CoreWeave is weird, nor because of the people. It was because all of a sudden, people started treating me as some sort of authority on a topic that I had only been researching for a few months. Internally, I still felt like this somewhat average kid who wasn't smart enough to get in through the front door of the U of I, and yet people thought I was some kind of genius. How? 

The thing people don't see, and what most "geniuses" don't advertise, is how much struggle it takes, how much uncertainty, self-doubt, how many stupid things you say, how often you're proven dead wrong, before you come to a place of adequacy where you tend to say 51% smart things and 49% stupid things. Stupidity is part of the process. Sure, it helps a LOT if you're humble early on in your career (unlike me) because you can learn so much faster, but you have to feel okay with being wrong, looking foolish, and being the idiot in the room before you can become competent.

----------------

I went to CoreWeave's engineering offsite in Seattle a few months ago. I had been working there for about three months at the time trying to make ye ol' Containers as a Service work (albeit specifically on making it work with GPUs, which is really fucking difficult because GPUs suck and are horrible). I'm the only person in the entire company working on this specific problem. The CTO Peter Salinski comes on stage in front of the _entire_ company, and one of the very first words out of his mouth were something like "one of the things I'm most excited for is the introduction of gVisor and Kata Containers into our infrastructure." Of course, those were the two things I was exclusively working on. Hearing the CTO of one the most important AI infrastructure company on the _planet_, in his first breath saying he's excited about what I am working on, did not compute.

I smiled a little, it wasn't a huge deal, but the aftermath of it made me realize that the reality of my career has far outpaced the feelings I have on it. People don't see how much I doubt my knowledge, how much I question my assumptions, how little I really know in the grand scheme of things. Oh yeah I'm just so humble I guess. It reminds me of this Weird Al Yankovic song _Amish Paradise_ where he sings:

> Think you're really righteous?
> 
> Think you're pure in heart?
> 
> Well, I know I'm a million times as humble as thou art
> 
> I'm the pious guy the little Amlettes wanna be like
> 
> On my knees day and night, scorin' points for the afterlife

If you had shown seventeen-year-old me this scene where I'm physically at today: a trailer beside an alpine river, a laptop connected to satellites overhead, coworkers a thousand miles away trusting me with problems I once barely understood, I wouldn’t have believed you. The strange thing is that I still don’t fully understand how I got here. Maybe nobody does. You follow your curiosity, you try to treat people well, you work hard, and every once in a while someone gives you more trust than you think you’ve earned. Then one day you find yourself sitting beside an alpine river wondering how on earth you got here.
