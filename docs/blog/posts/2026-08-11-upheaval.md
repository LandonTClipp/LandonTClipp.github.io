Far be it from me to be religious in the traditional sense. Those who know me know my embattled history with religion in my younger years. Still, I can't help but draw upon the ancient stories of the past to seek wisdom and parallels with the trials I face in life.

In the book of Exodus, the Israelites did not leave Egypt because they knew what came next. They left because they could no longer remain where they were. Generations of Israelites had lived in Egypt until a new Pharaoh came to see their growing population as a threat. He subjected them to forced labor, ordered increasingly brutal measures against them, and eventually commanded that their newborn sons be killed. Their suffering became so great that the story says their cries rose to God, who called Moses to lead them out of Egypt.

<!-- more -->

What followed is one of the foundational stories of Western civilization: Moses confronting Pharaoh, the plagues, the first Passover, and finally the hurried departure of an entire people from the only country most of them had ever known. Pharaoh changed his mind and pursued them. Trapped between his army and the sea, the Israelites watched the waters part before them. They crossed, the sea closed behind them, and Egypt, the place where they had suffered, but also the place they had known, was suddenly inaccessible.

On the other side was freedom, but also a vast and unforgiving desert.

The Israelites had been delivered from something terrible, yet almost immediately they began to struggle with what they had lost. They became hungry and thirsty. They were frightened. They quarreled with Moses. At times they wondered whether they would have been better off remaining in Egypt. Faced with the uncertainty of the wilderness, even captivity could acquire the strange comfort of familiarity.

The promised land did not appear over the next hill. And so they wandered for forty years.

So it has been with my life over the last few months. I had been in a relationship with a woman I loved for 8 years. We eventually married. We had a wonderful home, wonderful friends, shared memories, and a shared future. 

I eventually left that marriage because I felt like it could no longer be healthy. In doing so, I left the entire architecture of my life. I left the city we lived in, my friends and family who were close by. I left my home. I left the daily rhythms of being a husband. I left behind possessions, traditions, familiar places, and an entire future that had existed so vividly in my imagination that I had begun to experience it almost as a memory: anniversaries that would never happen, places we would never visit together, and an old age beside someone I had once assumed would always be there.

Nobody banished me from that life. I chose to leave it. Yet in some sense, I feel exiled by it. The country I inhabited still exists somewhere behind me, but I cannot return to it. Even if I physically went back, the life that existed there is gone.

Like the Israelites looking backward toward Egypt, I came upon an uncomfortable truth: you can desperately miss a place you know you needed to leave. And as it sometimes is in the desert, I have recently happened upon mirages, promises of relief from my pain and torment. Yet as with all illusions, they disappeared from my sight like smoke in the wind. How enticing those mirages are. Running towards them makes you feel as if the days of hunger and thirst are behind you, that you have finally made it to the promised land. 

Watching the illusion scatter before your eyes comes with it a pain almost worse than the exile itself. At least I had seen the exile coming. I had contemplated it, feared it, and ultimately chosen it. A mirage is different. You don’t recognize it as an illusion until you reach for it and find nothing there. Its disappearance dashes your hope of relief, and you realize you're back in the same oppressive place as you started.

As I'm sure it was with the Israelites, people cope with the endless desert with different philosophies. Most begin their exodus with a belief that the promised land lies somewhere beyond the hill. After wandering for years and years, some begin to doubt whether the promised land even exists at all. Others cling to a faith that the powers that be will deliver them from their suffering. Others find solace in making the desert their home because continuing to wander becomes too painful. 

I'm a stubborn bastard, and now that I've come to terms with my grief and the situation I find myself in, I will wander as long as I must. There will be water ahead, and there will be mirages. I may not always know the difference. The desert will teach me what it may. I cannot know how long it lasts, or what waits beyond it. I only know what I must do tomorrow. 

I will not make the desert my home. 

I will not make permanent camp. 

I do not belong here.

I will keep fucking walking.
