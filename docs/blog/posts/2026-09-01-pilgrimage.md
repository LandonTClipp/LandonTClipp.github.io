It's not even the normal time of year for reflection on what has happened in the last twelve months, but these last four months have compressed within them what feels like a lifetime, so reflection seems unavoidable. I began this year with one measure of hope and another measure of deep dissatisfaction. I still had it in my head last January that life would go my way, that the plan I had devised for myself, for my small family of two, would unfold more or less accordingly.

Oh, how life likes to utterly fuck things up.

<!-- more -->

> A man's heart deviseth his way: but the Lord directeth his steps.

I don't necessarily write for others, so I won't do the responsible thing and summarize everything that has happened. Clues can be found scattered through the poetry and essays I've written this year, some of them not at all reticent or mysterious. Suffice it to say that the life I thought I was living came apart with astonishing speed. I found myself in the midst of upheaval, grief, and heartbreak unlike anything I had experienced before. Worse than the pain itself was the feeling of being trapped inside it: circling the same memories, rehearsing the same imagined futures, bargaining endlessly with things that had already happened and things that might never happen.

There has always been one method I reach for that effectively and unfailingly helps me process grief: solitude in the deep wilderness.

So it was time for a pilgrimage.

And how serendipitous that I had recently found myself living in a state where there is quite a *lot* of wilderness.

I had known for weeks that this was coming. I wanted to disappear for a while into a place quiet enough that there would be nothing left to distract me from whatever it was I needed to hear. I knew I was suffering, and I knew some part of that suffering came from my refusal to accept what life had put in front of me. So I went into the mountains with no particular expectation of being healed, only with the intention of listening, and with the suspicion that I was not going to like what I heard.

## Solitude

I hooked up my 19-foot camper trailer to my truck and headed into the Mosquito Range in central Colorado. There I found exactly what I had been looking for: a patch of solitude with no one around.

![](https://f005.backblazeb2.com/file/landons-blog/assets/posts/2026-09-01-pilgrimage/IMG_1415.jpeg)

I should clarify that this was not exactly an ascetic pilgrimage. I was not wandering barefoot through the desert or sleeping beneath a wool blanket in the rain. I had all the modern amenities a man of fine taste might reasonably require: electricity, running water, hot showers (brief and infrequent, but hot nonetheless), refrigeration, a furnace for the cold mountain nights, and enough internet to keep the corporate bosses happy. The creature comforts were welcome, of course, but I justified them by telling myself they allowed me to remain out there indefinitely, undistracted by discomfort and with little reason to retreat into civilization.

I worked my day job that Friday. When I finished, I went outside, sat down, and watched the sky for hours.

There was no particular agenda. I watched the wind move through the grass, distant storms drag themselves across the mountains, and the sky slowly change color as the sun disappeared behind the peaks. For once I wasn't trying very hard to understand anything. I was simply there to witness it.

The next morning I did much the same thing, except this time I stayed outside for nearly twelve hours. I watched. I read. I thought. I grieved. And, despite all my talk of surrender, I fought furiously against the idea that there might truly be nothing I could do to change what had happened. I begged God. I pleaded with him. I bargained. I accused him of cruelty. I asked him to give me what I wanted, or at least to explain why I could not have it.

And yet, in almost the same breath, I found myself asking for something else: if there was a lesson in all of this, let me hear it. It came, or at least I felt it come, during the second spectacular alpenglow sunset.

The mountains were glowing in that impossible red-orange light, the clouds above them catching fire, and for a while I simply stared. Whatever voice I had been waiting for did not arrive as words so much as a realization.

For months I had lived almost entirely somewhere else. In the past, replaying what had happened. In the future, imagining what might happen, what could still be repaired, what might yet be lost. Very little of my suffering existed in the few square feet of earth where I was actually sitting. But the mountains, the sunset, and the wind did. And so, for a little while, I let everything else go.

![](https://f005.backblazeb2.com/file/landons-blog/assets/posts/2026-09-01-pilgrimage/IMG_1494.jpeg)

One thing I have come to understand about the natural world is that it does not operate according to our clock. You can spend days in the wilderness and see nothing remarkable. The rain may pour, the animals may hide, and the stars may remain behind a veil of clouds. Those too impatient to wait will leave before the beauty returns. And it always does return, just rarely when we demand it.

## Weston Pass

I decided, more or less at random, to drive up Weston Pass. I knew nothing about it except for the fact that it connected the towns of Fairplay and Leadville together. At the crest I pulled over, found a trail climbing away from the road, and followed it toward the top of the ridge. Once again, I found myself stunned by what was waiting there.

Only after I reached the top did I learn that there would be a lunar eclipse that night, beginning in less than two hours. I had come up the pass completely ignorant of it, but now there was nothing to do but stay.

I watched as the shadow slowly moved across the moon, as if pieces of it were being swallowed by the night. Then, just as totality began, the clouds rolled in.

For a moment the symbolism felt almost too obvious. Something beautiful had appeared unexpectedly, and just before I could witness it in its entirety, it disappeared from view. But the totality was still happening somewhere beyond the clouds. I simply could not see it anymore.

Strangely, I wasn't disappointed. I had not gone up the mountain expecting an eclipse. I hadn’t planned for the eclipse. I didn’t even know it was happening until I got up there. So what exactly was I supposed to be angry about? I got lucky. I saw part of something beautiful, and then the clouds took the rest.

On the long drive back to camp, as if the mountains had decided I had not been given quite enough for one evening, a giant stepped into the road. He stopped and turned to look at me.

![](https://f005.backblazeb2.com/file/landons-blog/assets/posts/2026-09-01-pilgrimage/IMG_1544.jpeg)

This was, in fact, the third or fourth time I had encountered this particular individual. There seemed to be a pair of them living somewhere in the valley, and for whatever reason, our paths kept crossing.

## Decalibron

There was something deeply satisfying about moving from summit to summit. From below, the whole loop looks absurd: four mountains piled one behind another, each one asking for more climbing just when you think you've surely done enough. But mountains have a way of making the future irrelevant. You do not climb four fourteeners. You climb the stretch of rock immediately in front of you. Then another. Then another. Eventually you look behind yourself and discover that the impossible thing has somehow become the place you are standing.

I think this is one of the reasons I have grown so attached to climbing mountains. They are mercilessly present. The summit may motivate you, but thinking about the summit does almost nothing to get you there. Your body can only take the step it is taking now. The mountain does not care about your plans, your grief, your impatience, or how badly you wish the trail were easier. It gives you exactly one thing to do: continue. And so I continued.

By the time I reached Lincoln, much of the world had fallen away beneath me. The valleys looked impossibly far below, the neighboring peaks stretched endlessly into the distance, and for a little while my problems seemed to have assumed their proper scale. They had not disappeared. I had not solved them. They were simply no longer the largest things in the world.

After Lincoln came Cameron, and beyond Cameron waited Democrat. At one point I stopped and stared up at Mt. Democrat looming ahead of me. From where I stood, it looked enormous. The trail seemed to rise forever, switchbacking up a wall of rock toward a summit that felt impossibly far away. I remember wondering how in the hell I was supposed to get through it. My legs were tired, the air was thin, and all I could see was the enormous thing still standing between me and where I wanted to go. Then I turned around.

Behind me stretched the three mountains I had already climbed, rising thousands of feet from the valley floor beneath the saddle where I stood. I had been so fixated on the ascent in front of me that I had forgotten to notice how far I had already come. And there was something almost embarrassingly obvious in that moment. I had spent so much of this year staring at what remained, the dismantling of an old life, the grief still unresolved, the life I still had to rebuild, that I rarely turned around long enough to see the ground already beneath my feet. I had already climbed farther than Democrat without realizing it. It still looked like a pain in the ass. It was still steep. My legs still hurt. None of that had changed. But when I turned around, I realized I had already done more than the thing I was afraid of doing next. I was standing on the evidence that I could.

## Wheeler Lake

![](https://f005.backblazeb2.com/file/landons-blog/assets/posts/2026-09-01-pilgrimage/IMG_1625.jpeg)

From the summit of Lincoln I noticed a lake tucked into the valley far beneath me. I knew nothing about it. I didn't know its name, how difficult the trail was, or whether there was anything particularly remarkable waiting at the end of it. I only knew that I could see a trail winding toward it, and I wanted to know where it went. So the next morning I went.

There was no summit to conquer this time, no particular achievement waiting for me at the end. I was tired from the day before and my legs reminded me frequently of the four mountains I had just climbed. But I followed the trail anyway, through the valley I had looked down upon only a day earlier.

Something about that pleased me enormously. Yesterday I had stood thousands of feet above this place and wondered what it was. Today I was inside it. The world had begun to feel expansive again.

For months my attention had been pulled relentlessly toward the things I had lost: the life that did not happen, the people who were no longer beside me, the future that had vanished behind the clouds. Grief has a peculiar way of shrinking the universe until the lost thing becomes almost the entirety of it. But there was a lake I had never seen. There was a trail I had never walked. There were mountains beyond mountains whose names I did not know. Suddenly there were things ahead of me again.

## Patagonia

Somewhere in the middle of this trip, I booked a 15 day trip to Patagonia. The idea did not originally belong to me. Someone I had briefly known spoke about Patagonia with such reverence that the place lodged itself somewhere in my imagination. They had been there many times before and described it as one of those corners of the world that rearranges your understanding of beauty. Our time together ended almost as quickly as it began, but the idea remained. There was something strangely appropriate about that. Not everything that enters our lives is meant to remain there. Sometimes a person stays only long enough to point toward a road you might never otherwise have noticed.

On February 20th of next year, I will fly south toward the end of the earth and spend two weeks trekking through Patagonia. I will cross into a landscape of mountains larger and stranger than the ones that have already come to mean so much to me. I will turn thirty-one there. If everything goes according to plan, I will wake up on my birthday somewhere thousands of miles from the life I once believed I would be living. There is also no small irony in writing *if everything goes according to plan.* I have learned a thing or two about plans this year.

For months I had experienced the future primarily as a place of absence. I thought about what would not happen, who would not be there, the family that would not exist in the form I had imagined, the years that would unfold differently than I had planned. The future had become almost indistinguishable from loss. Then, quite suddenly, there was Patagonia.

And then I started thinking about the absurdity of it. There is an entire continent down there I have never seen. Mountains I don’t know the names of. Trails I’ve never heard of. People I haven’t met yet. Hell, even the sun will be on the wrong side of the sky.

The person I cared for may have pointed me towards those mountains, but I do not have to carry this person there with me. I can be grateful for the direction without demanding that the person who showed it to me accompany me down the road. The gift remains even after the giver is gone.

I believe that is what this pilgrimage is teaching me. The future does not owe me the life I imagined. It only continues opening. There are still mountains beyond mountains, and now I want to see them.
