There is something peculiar about the human condition. We are, as far as we know, the only creatures on Earth capable of contemplating the tragedy of our own existence. We are aware that we are alive, that we will someday die, and that everything we have ever loved will eventually disappear. We can agonize over events that happened decades ago, fear circumstances that may never come to pass, and construct elaborate stories about ourselves that we spend our entire lives trying to defend.

We are creatures capable of extraordinary beauty, compassion, and creativity. Yet we are also creatures uniquely capable of becoming prisoners of our own minds.

<!-- more -->

The story of humanity's original sin in the Book of Genesis is not merely a story about disobedience, but something considerably more profound. It is an allegory for the emergence of human self-consciousness. The Fall of Man represents the moment humanity became aware of itself as something separate from the world. Our expulsion from paradise was not a punishment imposed upon us, but the inevitable psychological consequence of learning to see ourselves as individuals.

Paradise was never a physical place, but rather a mental mode of being in deep harmonious communion with the natural world.


## The Invention of the Self

For hundreds of millions of years, life evolved on Earth without anything resembling human civilization. Countless generations of organisms lived, reproduced, struggled, and died. Through natural selection, increasingly sophisticated nervous systems emerged, allowing organisms to construct progressively more elaborate representations of their surroundings.

Eventually, among the ancestors of humanity, something extraordinary developed. Our brains became capable of modeling not merely the immediate environment, but increasingly complex relationships between ourselves, other organisms, and events separated across time.

One influential theory in modern neuroscience, known as _predictive processing_, proposes that the brain does not passively observe reality. Rather, it continuously constructs models of the world, generating predictions about incoming sensory information and updating those predictions in response to new evidence.

What we experience as reality is shaped by an extraordinary process of interpretation. We do not simply see the world. Our brains construct an experience of it. This ability has enormous evolutionary advantages. An organism capable of anticipating threats, remembering past experiences, imagining future possibilities, and understanding the intentions of others possesses tremendous advantages over one that cannot.

As human cognitive abilities became more sophisticated, our ancestors developed increasingly elaborate capacities for planning, cooperation, language, and social understanding. But consider what might happen when a brain becomes sophisticated enough to model not only the world, but itself. It begins to construct a representation of its own existence within that world. It recognizes itself as an individual, separate from other individuals. It becomes capable of imagining how others perceive it. It develops an understanding of its own history and begins to anticipate its future. It discovers that it is mortal. 

Somewhere in this extraordinary process, something resembling the human ego emerges. The ego, in this sense, is not simply arrogance or selfishness. It is the mind's representation of itself: the collection of memories, beliefs, identities, expectations, and judgments that together constitute our experience of being an individual. It is the story we tell ourselves about who we are. This story, in the case of collective human experience, more often than not demonstrates itself to be a fantastical misrepresentation of the true relationship between ourselves and the natural world. Humanity's extraordinary congnitive development was equally the event in which an entirely new category of suffering came to be.

An animal may experience pain, fear, grief, or distress. Some animals possess meaningful degrees of self-awareness, and we should be cautious about assuming that their inner lives are simple. But humans appear particularly capable of constructing elaborate narratives about suffering that extend far beyond the immediate experience. We can suffer because of something that happened twenty years ago, because of something we imagine might happen twenty years from now. We can suffer because we believe someone dislikes us, because we regret the person we once were, or because we fear becoming someone we do not wish to be. We can experience humiliation without anyone witnessing it. We can experience rejection without anyone rejecting us. We can experience terror at the contemplation of our own inevitable death while sitting perfectly safe beneath a beautiful summer sky.

Our brains have become so extraordinarily sophisticated at modeling the world that the models themselves have become a source of suffering. Think about it: why does private humiliation affect us so much? Why does an imagined future in which our crush rejects us debase our sense of worthiness? Why does fear of failure prevent us from striving towards the career we believe we ought to have? In these cases and more, our compulsive and maligniant tendency to model the past and the future creates for us a pain over a situation that has not even occurred. This pain arises out of nothing, and so forth people live their lives.

## The Garden of Eden

_The Knowledge of Good and Evil._

In the Book of Genesis, Adam and Eve inhabit a paradise in which their needs are provided for. They exist in harmony with their surroundings, without shame, without the burden of labor, and without the knowledge of their eventual mortality. They are naked, but they experience no embarrassment. They exist, seemingly, without the psychological burden of judging their own existence.

At the center of the garden stands the Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil. God warns them not to eat its fruit. When they eventually do, something terribly consequential happens. Genesis 3:7 describes the immediate consequence:

> Then the eyes of both were opened, and they knew that they were naked.


Notice what does not happen. Adam and Eve do not suddenly become cruel. They do not develop murderous intentions. They do not immediately become consumed by greed or malice. Instead, the first consequence of eating the fruit is that they become aware of themselves. They recognize their nakedness, experience shame, and attempt to conceal their bodies.

Consider what must happen within a mind for shame to become possible. One must possess a representation of oneself. One must be capable of imagining oneself from the perspective of another person. And one must be capable of evaluating that representation against some imagined standard. Shame requires a self that can be judged.

Before eating the fruit, Adam and Eve possessed bodies, but they do not appear troubled by their bodies. Afterward, they become capable of regarding themselves as objects of judgment. Something has changed in their relationship to themselves.

The transformation continues when God enters the garden and Adam hides from him. In Genesis 3:10, Adam explains:

> I heard thy voice in the garden, and I was afraid, because I was naked; and I hid myself.

Fear. Shame. Self-evaluation. Concealment. These are among the defining characteristics of the human psychological condition. This is the true significance of the knowledge represented by the forbidden fruit.
The knowledge of good and evil is not merely the acquisition of moral understanding. It also represents the emergence of the evaluative mind itself: the capacity to divide experience into categories, to judge ourselves and the world, and to understand our existence in relation to imagined possibilities.

Before the Fall, Adam and Eve simply exist. After the Fall, they know that they exist. That knowledge changes everything.

## The Price of Awareness

Humanity's greatest cognitive achievement may also have been the source of its deepest existential suffering. Imagine for a moment an organism that experiences the world without constructing a persistent narrative about its own identity. It encounters hunger and seeks food. It experiences danger and seeks safety. It encounters favorable circumstances and responds accordingly. This does not mean that the organism is incapable of emotion, intelligence, memory, or suffering. Nor does it mean that nonhuman animals are entirely confined to the present moment. But there is a meaningful distinction between experiencing suffering and constructing an elaborate psychological identity around suffering.

Human beings do both. We do not merely experience loss. We contemplate what that loss means about our lives.
We do not merely experience rejection. We ask whether that rejection reveals something fundamental about our worth. We do not merely fear death. We recognize that death is inevitable, imagine the disappearance of everything we love, and sometimes spend years agonizing over the implications.

The mind becomes capable of creating suffering even in the absence of an immediate threat. It discovers the future, and with it, anxiety. It discovers the past, and with it, regret. It discovers itself, and with it, shame.

The Garden of Eden is not necessarily a geographical location from which humanity was physically expelled, it represents a psychological condition that humanity could no longer inhabit. Once we acquired the capacity to understand ourselves as separate individuals, the world could never again appear quite as it had before.

We had eaten from the Tree of Knowledge, our eyes had been opened, and paradise was forever lost.

## An Ancient Recognition

The psychoanalyst and philosopher Erich Fromm explored a remarkably similar interpretation in his 1956 book _The Art of Loving_. Fromm argued that the emergence of human reason and self-awareness created a profound separation between humanity and the natural world.

As human beings became conscious of themselves as individuals, they developed an awareness of their separateness from other people, from nature, and from the world around them. They recognized their vulnerability, their isolation, and their ultimate powerlessness against death. For Fromm, this separateness produced a fundamental existential anxiety. Human beings were driven by a longing to overcome their isolation, to recover a sense of union with something beyond themselves. Love, in Fromm's understanding, represented one of the most profound responses to this predicament. 

There are also fascinating parallels between the Garden of Eden and the ancient Mesopotamian Epic of Gilgamesh. In that story, Enkidu initially lives among the animals, naked and integrated into their world. Following his encounter with human sexuality and civilization, he acquires new understanding, but loses his former relationship with the animals. He gains something extraordinary, yet something equally extraordinary is taken from him. The resemblance to Adam and Eve is difficult to ignore.

Both stories explore a transformation from an animal-like condition of innocence into a distinctly human existence characterized by knowledge, self-awareness, and separation from nature.

I find it fascinating that ancient civilizations, thousands of years before modern neuroscience, developed stories that can be interpreted as meditations on the psychological consequences of becoming human. This does not mean their authors possessed a scientific understanding of human evolution, nor that these stories were consciously intended as accounts of evolutionary history. 

The ancient storytellers recognized, through introspection and observation, a fundamental tension within human existence. We possess the extraordinary gift of understanding the world, but that understanding can separate us from the immediacy of experiencing it. We are creatures of nature who have become capable of regarding ourselves as strangers within it. 

Yet if this is a fundamental predicament of human consciousness, another question naturally follows: Did ancient humanity develop ways of overcoming it? Did our ancestors discover techniques for temporarily escaping the confines of the individual, egoistic self, recovering a sense of unity with the world, and confronting the terror of their own mortality?

## The Mysteries of Eleusis

For roughly a thousand years, ancient Greeks and, later, Romans traveled to the sanctuary of Eleusis, near Athens, to participate in one of the most sacred religious ceremonies of the ancient Mediterranean world.
These ceremonies, known as the Eleusinian Mysteries, were considered so sacred that their central revelations were protected by strict vows of secrecy. Initiates were forbidden from disclosing what they had witnessed. 

Although historians have reconstructed many aspects of the ceremonies, the precise nature of their climactic revelations remains unknown. What we do know is that the Mysteries revolved around the myth of Demeter, the goddess of agriculture, and her daughter Persephone. According to the myth, Persephone was abducted by Hades, ruler of the underworld. Demeter, devastated by the loss of her daughter, wandered the Earth searching for her. In her grief, she withdrew her divine blessings from the land. The fields became barren, the crops ceased to grow, and humanity faced starvation. 

Eventually, an arrangement was reached permitting Persephone to return to her mother for part of each year, while spending the remaining portion in the underworld. Their reunion restored fertility to the Earth. The story became intimately associated with the cycles of nature: growth and decay, abundance and scarcity, separation and reunion, life and death.

The story is not merely about the changing seasons, it is about the inevitability of loss and the possibility of renewal. Persephone descends into the underworld, but she is not lost forever. Demeter experiences terrible grief, but that grief is not the final condition of existence. Life retreats beneath the surface of the Earth, only to emerge again.

The ancient Greeks recognized that the natural world does not exist in a perpetual state of flourishing. It moves through cycles. Spring gives way to summer. Summer gives way to autumn. Autumn gives way to winter.
And winter, eventually, gives way to spring. Death and decay are not aberrations imposed upon nature, they are inseparable from its rhythms.

The Eleusinian Mysteries offered initiates an opportunity to experience this truth not merely as an intellectual proposition, but as something felt within the depths of consciousness. The ceremonies included preparation, purification, fasting, procession, and secret rites associated with Demeter and Persephone. Modern interpretations sometimes describe the initiatory experience as a symbolic descent into darkness, a confrontation with death or divine mystery, and a subsequent return into light.

The symbolism is hard to ignore. The initiate confronts the darkness by descending into Hades, encounters the mystery of death, and ascends back into the world transformed.

The significance of these experiences was apparently extraordinary. The Roman statesman Cicero, himself an initiate, praised the Mysteries for teaching people not only how to live more authentically, but how to face death with greater hope. The Homeric Hymn to Demeter similarly associates initiation with a more favorable destiny after death.

The rites were not simply celebrations of agricultural fertility. They offered a response to one of the most terrible discoveries made possible by human consciousness: the knowledge that we must die. Through confronting that knowledge, they offered a way to become less imprisoned by it.

## The Sacred Potion

There is another element of the Eleusinian Mysteries that makes them particularly fascinating in light of modern neuroscience. At some point during the ceremonies, initiates consumed a sacred drink known as kykeon. The Homeric Hymn to Demeter describes a preparation of barley, water, and pennyroyal, an aromatic herb belonging to the mint family.

For centuries, the exact nature of the sacramental drink and its possible effects have been subjects of speculation. In 1978, the ethnomycologist R. Gordon Wasson, chemist Albert Hofmann, and classicist Carl Ruck proposed a remarkable hypothesis. They suggested that the kykeon may have contained psychoactive compounds derived from ergot, a fungus that infects cereal grains. This was particularly interesting because ergot contains alkaloids chemically related to compounds used in the synthesis of LSD.

The hypothesis was subsequently explored and popularized by Brian Muraresku in his book The Immortality Key. According to this interpretation, the extraordinary revelations experienced during the Mysteries may have been facilitated, at least in part, by a psychedelic sacrament. The initiates may have entered an altered state of consciousness in which their ordinary experience of individual identity became less dominant. They may have confronted the apparent dissolution of the self, experienced something resembling psychological death, and subsequently emerged with a transformed relationship to existence.

There is something profoundly suggestive about this possibility. Imagine an ancient Greek, accustomed to the ordinary burdens of human existence, undergoing a carefully prepared initiation. The participant has fasted, traveled to a sacred sanctuary, and become immersed in rituals that evoke death, separation, and reunion. The ordinary structures through which that person understands the world may begin to loosen. The distinction between self and nature becomes less rigid. The fear of death may temporarily recede as the initiate experiences consciousness in a manner fundamentally different from ordinary waking life. And upon returning to everyday existence, the participant carries the memory of an experience in which the boundaries of the individual self were no longer clearly distinguishable.

What's clear is that ancient civilizations recognized the transformative power of temporarily stepping outside of normal consciousness. What a tragedy it was that the collective egoistic mind-sickness, riding upon the horse called Christianity (just one of many steeds it would employ), eventually destroyed this deeply profound ritual. It would be one of many movements, and certainly not the last, that sought to snuff out the deep philosophical and spiritual truths these rituals manifested for its initiates.

## The Death and Rebirth of the Self

The connection between Genesis and Eleusis becomes particularly interesting when we consider their contrasting relationships to death. In Genesis, Adam and Eve acquire knowledge and become aware of their vulnerability. Their expulsion from Eden introduces them to a world of labor, suffering, and mortality. That acquisition of knowledge marks the end of innocence.

In the Eleusinian Mysteries, by contrast, participants appear to have deliberately confronted the mysteries of mortality. Rather than attempting to preserve innocence, they entered a sacred experience designed to transform their relationship to death.

These stories can be understood as describing two different movements of consciousness. Genesis describes the emergence of the self and its separation from the world. Eleusis offers the possibility of transforming that separation through a symbolic encounter with death and renewal. 

The beautiful symmetry between these stories is hard to deny. In Eden, humanity acquires the knowledge that makes existential suffering possible. At Eleusis, humanity developed a ritual for confronting that suffering without being destroyed by it. The psychedelic component of the Eleusis rituals likely involved profound ego dissolution that separated the initiate's ordinary sense of being a separate, persistent individual. Drawing from the likely modern analogues of psilocybin or LSD, the self does not necessarily disappear in any literal sense. Rather, the familiar psychological processes that maintain our ordinary sense of identity become less dominant. Perhaps too, does this dissolution of self become a durable state post-ritual.

The person may experience a profound sense of unity with the surrounding world, a weakening of the distinction between subject and object, or an apparent transcendence of the boundaries of individual existence. For someone terrified of death, such an experience could carry extraordinary psychological significance. The fear of death depends, in part, upon our identification with a self that we know must eventually disappear. If that identification temporarily dissolves, the relationship between consciousness and mortality may begin to feel different.

These experiences allow a person to recognize that the ordinary narrative of the self is not identical to the entirety of conscious experience. The individual returns from the experience still knowing that death is inevitable, but the psychological meaning of that knowledge may have changed. The terror of annihilation might give way to a feeling of participation in something larger than oneself. Not necessarily something supernatural. Perhaps simply the vast, interconnected processes of nature from which life emerges and into which it eventually returns.

## When Life Became Unlivable

The Eleusinian Mysteries eventually disappeared during the transformation of the Roman Empire. Their decline occurred alongside the rise of Christianity and the increasing suppression of traditional pagan religious practices. But one incident from the fourth century reveals just how profoundly important the Mysteries remained to their participants. The Roman emperor Valentinian I introduced restrictions on nocturnal religious ceremonies, which conveniently matched the profile of the Eleusinian Mysteries.

According to the ancient historian Zosimus, a Roman official named Vettius Agorius Praetextatus protested that preventing the Greeks from performing their sacred Mysteries would make life unbearable for them. The ceremonies were so essential to their religious and cultural existence that their loss threatened something fundamental. Valentinian subsequently permitted the traditional rites to continue despite his broader restrictions. The Mysteries would not survive the remaining decades of religious transformation, but this intervention reveals how deeply they were valued. 

Greek civiliation had produced some of humanity's greatest achievements in philosophy, mathematics, political thought, architecture, and the arts. They developed elaborate systems of rational inquiry and attempted to understand nature through observation, argument, and reason. Yet at the center of their religious culture stood an extraordinary ritual concerned with experiences that apparently could not be adequately communicated through ordinary language. A ritual of mystery, of death and renewal. A ritual that could not simply be replaced by philosophical debate.

The ancient Greeks recognized that reason, however powerful, could not resolve every question humanity faced. We can understand intellectually that death is inevitable without reconciling with that eventuality. We can understand that we are part of nature without witnessing that connectedness. We can know that our psychological identities are artificial constructions without ever becoming free of their influence. Knowledge of these things alone does not procure lasting transformation. It seems that human beings often must genuinely _experience_ these truths before truly understanding it beyond a cerebral level. Psychedelics, being a tool for experiencing altered states of consciouness, have long been a method for dissolving our preconceived models of being.

## The Dissolution of the Ego

My fascination with these ideas is not entirely intellectual. There is of course, as I mentioned, a substantial difference between intellectual knowledge and being a first-hand witness. Earlier this month, I underwent a carefully prepared psilocybin experience that profoundly altered my understanding of consciousness. During the journey, I encountered a stillness unlike anything I had previously known. The ordinary hierarchy of my consciousness seemed to disolve. The incessant activity of thought, interpretation, judgement, and self-reference all became absolutely halted.

I remember feeling as though my consciousness had become like an undisturbed alpine lake. Even the act of attempting to form coherent thought, speaking itself, felt like someone violently striking the perfectly still water. What struck me most was not the presence of some extraordinary new faculty of perception, but rather the inherent absence of something that had ordinarily been there. It was as if I had peeled back layers of ordinary compulsive thinking and witnessed the truth of consciousness that had always lied underneath. The sense of peace was profound.

There is an influential theory of psychedelic action known as REBUS, an acronym for Relaxed Beliefs Under Psychedelics, proposed by neuroscientist Robin Carhart-Harris and colleagues. The theory suggests that psychedelics temporarily relax the influence of certain high-level beliefs and expectations that ordinarily constrain cognition and perception. These beliefs help organize our experience of reality. They allow us to navigate the world efficiently and maintain relatively stable understandings of ourselves and our surroundings. But they can also become rigid. We often become so thoroughly identified with our models of reality that we lose sight of their provisional nature. Under psychedelics, these deeply entrenched patterns become less dominant, paving way for alternative ways of experiencing the world and oneself to emerge.

Consciousness is not a single, fixed condition. The self is not necessarily as permanent or fundamental as it appears. The ordinary mind, with its endless preoccupation with identity, memory, judgement, and anticipation, is only one of many possible ways of experiencing existence.

The experience of ego dissolution resembles to me, at least phenomenologically, the psychological innocence symbolied by paradise, by the Garden of Eden. Psychedelics help us experience an inhabited reality whereby the past becomes less insistent, the future becomes less threatening, and the endless compulsion to evaluate, interpret, defend, and explain oneself begins to subside. I don't believe psychedelics hold a monopoly on these kinds of realizations. In fact, I believe many of these realizations had begun to manifest for me through my extended meditations in wilderness. Psychedelics are like a tool that crack open any remaining vestige of the old identity you may be clinging on to.[^1]

## The Wilderness and the Self

Over the past several months, I have spent an extraordinary amount of time alone in the mountains of Colorado. I have hiked across alpine ridgelines, wandered through forests of golden aspens, watched storms gather over mountain passes, and spent long evenings alone in my trailer beneath the stars. Somewhat to my surprise, I have rarely felt lonely.

I initially expected solitude to become burdensome. After all, I had recently experienced the collapse of a relationship that had defined much of my adult life. For months, my mind had been occupied with the past. I revisited conversations, analyzed decisions, wondered what another person thought of me, and agonized over questions that might never receive satisfactory answers. My suffering was real. The events that produced it were real, but I gradually began to recognize how much of that suffering was sustained by the stories my mind continued telling about those events.

I was not merely grieving the loss of a relationship, I was grieving the collapse of a narrative about who I was, who I would become, and what my life was supposed to mean. Out in the depths of the wilderness, something began to move. I could sit beside a mountain stream and watch the water tumble over the stones without feeling compelled to interpret the experience in relation to my personal history. I could admire the extraordinary colors of an autumn aspen forest without needing another person to witness them. I could simply be there to witness it myself.

The nature around me had no conception of who I was, where I came from, what past traumas I was clinging to. It is radically, unremittingly, stubbornly present. Increasingly, I did not reach for the compulsion to remind myself of my past stories. It's profoundly liberating to exist in a place where identity is entirely irrelevant. You are not your job, your relationships, your failures, your tribulations, you are simply a human being standing beneath an enormous sky. And how disruptive it is for me to return to civilization where the collective sickness of humanity compulsively attempts to label and categorize you. It's unavoidable.

I wonder whether this is part of what humanity has long been searching for ever since its metaphorical expulsion from Eden. Not necessarily as a return to the ignorance of our ancestors, but a recovery of the capacity to inhabit the world without constantly being viewed through the lens of the egoistic, mind-identified self.

There is even something of the Eleusinian journey in the process of grief. There is the descent, in which the familiar structures of life collapse and we find ourselves wandering through a psychological underworld. There is the search, where we attempt to understand what has happened, what we have lost, and whether some part of our former lives can be recovered. Then, there is the ascent in which we eventually discover that life has continued in our absence, that beauty has survived our suffering, and that the world still possesses a capacity to astonish us.

The ascent does not undo the descent. Persephone must return to the underworld, the seasons continue their unwavering progression, and we too must eventually encouter suffering again. The purpose of the journey is not to eliminate suffering, but to have a relationship with it. The wisdom of the Mysteries was that darkness need not be the final condition of existence, that the winter of the soul is not necessarily permanent. Even after the most terrible of losses, the world retains its capacity for renewal.

## The Flaming Sword

After Adam and Eve eat from the Tree of Knowledge, they are expelled from the Garden of Eden. Genesis 3:24 describes what happens next:

> So he drove out the man; and he placed at the east of the garden of Eden Cherubims, and a flaming sword which turned every way, to keep the way of the tree of life.

The entrance to paradise is forever guarded and humanity cannot return. In the traditional religious interpretation, this represents the consequence of disobedience and humanity's separation from God. The analogy is deeper than this (what is in my opinion a shallow) interpretation. Once consciousness has become aware of itself, it cannot simply become unaware again. We cannot unlearn our mortality, nor erase our understanding of suffering. We cannot permanently forget that we are separate individuals living within an indifferent and incomprehensibly vast universe. We cannot turn back the clock to again become the innocent creatures that have never contemplated their own existence. This knowledge cannot be undone, and the garden cannot be reentered by retracing our steps.

And yet, I do not believe that humanity is condemned to suffer forever beneath the weight of its own consciousness. Before the Fall, Adam and Even inhabited the world without the burden of reflective self-consciousness. After the Fall, they became capable of reflection, judgement, shame, fear, and the construction of the egoistic identity. I do believe that consciousness can eventually develop a new relationship with itself. We can become aware of the very process by which our minds construct the experience of being someone. We can learn to experience the present moment directly, not because we have forsaken the memory of the past or pretended that the future will not one day become the _now_, but because we have learned that neither of those things must dominate our experience of being alive.

This is what the initiates at Eleusis were likely in part searching for. They did not come to escape the human condition by denying death. They entered into its mystery, confronted the darkness of the underworld, participated in the sacred drama of loss and reunion, and returned to the world with the hope that death was no longer something they needed to fear in quite the same way.

In Eden, the acquisition of knowledge produces exile. In Eleusis, the confrontation with mortality produces transformation. One story describes the terrible burden of becoming conscious, while the other suggests that consciousness can learn to bear that burden differently. First, we become aware of ourselves, then, we suffer beneat the weight of that awareness. Finally, we begin to discover that the self we have constructed is not the entirety of what we are. This would not be a return to Eden, but something different. 

The flaming sword still guards the entrance to Eden. There is no going back to the innocence of not knowing. I think our understanding of the nature of exile was misguided. Paradise was never truly lost when humanity acquired knowledge. Rather, it became hidden beneath the stories we began telling ourselves. The ancient Greeks, in their mysterious ceremonies of death and rebirth, discovered that the path to peace was not through the recovery of ignorance, but through the transformation of awareness.  

The journey does not lead backwards, it leads through the darkness and out the other side.
