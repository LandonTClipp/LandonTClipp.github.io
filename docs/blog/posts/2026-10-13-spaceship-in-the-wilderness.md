I'm writing this from a nineteen-foot camper trailer parked somewhere along Kebler Pass, west of Crested Butte, Colorado. It's October. The nights are getting cold, the aspens have turned brilliant shades of gold, and the mountains are preparing themselves for winter. Outside my window is a wilderness that has existed in some form for millions of years, almost entirely indifferent to the affairs of humanity.

Inside, I'm warm and comfortable. I have a propane furnace, two refrigerators, a week's worth of food, 41 gallons of fresh water, and four 100 amp-hour batteries charged by solar panels on the roof. I have a computer, an Apple Vision Pro, and a Starlink terminal that connects me to the internet through a constellation of satellites orbiting hundreds of miles above the Earth. I find this absolutely astonishing.

<!-- more -->

Yesterday, I drove through Schofield Pass, past the little town of Crystal, and onward through Marble before returning toward Crested Butte by way of Kebler Pass. The landscape was almost indescribably beautiful. Great mountains rose above enormous groves of aspens, their golden leaves catching the last light of autumn. I was so captivated that I decided to come back with my trailer and spend some time here.

By the time I found a campsite, darkness had already fallen. I could barely see my surroundings. I parked the trailer, settled in, switched on my electrical system, and connected to Starlink. Because I'm an insufferable nerd, I put on my Apple Vision Pro VR headset and started watching For All Mankind on Apple TV.

For those unfamiliar, For All Mankind is a science-fiction television series imagining an alternate history in which the space race never ended. Much of the show follows astronauts living and working in spacecraft and remote extraterrestrial outposts, separated from the familiar comforts of Earth, sustained by the machines and supplies they have brought with them. 

Somewhere in the middle of the episode I was watching, it struck me. I'm doing something remarkably similar. Not in the sense that camping in Colorado is remotely comparable to surviving on Mars, of course. Earth's atmosphere, gravity, and abundant natural resources are doing me some rather considerable favors. But conceptually, my little trailer is a spacecraft.

I carry my energy with me. I carry my food and water. I have systems that regulate the temperature of my living environment, manage waste, preserve food, and provide communications. My ability to remain here depends on the resources I've brought and my ability to manage them. For the next week, I could remain in this little bubble of civilization without returning to town. And tomorrow morning, I'll wake up, make coffee, open my laptop, and go to work. In the middle of the wilderness. How extraordinary is that?

## The Miracle of the Ordinary

![](https://f005.backblazeb2.com/file/landons-blog/assets/posts/2026-10-13-spaceship-in-the-wilderness/IMG_2356.jpeg)

I work as a systems engineer. I spend my days thinking about computers, virtualization, hardware, and the intricate machinery that makes modern computing possible. Consequently, I have at least some appreciation for the extraordinary complexity of the systems I'm using tonight.

Consider the chain of events necessary for me to access the internet from this remote mountain road. Approximately eight minutes ago, photons left the Sun and began traveling across ninety-three million miles of empty space. Some fraction of those photons eventually struck the solar panels mounted on my trailer, where semiconductor materials converted their energy into electricity. That electricity was stored chemically in batteries, ready to be released whenever I needed it. Those batteries now power a satellite terminal that uses a phased-array antenna to electronically direct radio signals toward spacecraft moving at roughly seventeen thousand miles per hour, hundreds of miles above my head. Those spacecraft belong to a constellation of thousands of satellites, each participating in an extraordinarily complex system of orbital mechanics, communications, routing, and network infrastructure. 

Information travels from my little trailer into space, back to Earth, through terrestrial networks, and eventually to the computers and services I want to reach. Meanwhile, the Apple Vision Pro on my face is tracking my eyes, hands, and head movements while rendering a convincing virtual environment around me. Within that environment, I can watch a television show about humans exploring the solar system, browse the internet, or interact with my computer as though I were sitting in front of an enormous collection of monitors.

All of this is happening in a tiny camper trailer, parked on a dirt road, miles from the nearest town. The entire arrangement is so thoroughly absurd that I scarcely know what to make of it. Yet perhaps the most extraordinary thing is not that any of this technology exists, it's that it works. Not occasionally. Not as some expensive scientific demonstration conducted by a team of researchers in carefully controlled conditions. It works reliably enough that I can simply take it for granted. I can park my trailer on the side of a mountain, switch on a few devices, and reasonably expect the whole arrangement to function. There are no technicians standing nearby to troubleshoot the satellite constellation. There are no mission controllers monitoring my electrical system. There is no engineering team helping me operate the computer strapped to my face. I simply turn everything on. And it works.

Humanity has accomplished something remarkable when technologies of such staggering complexity become so dependable that their operation is considered ordinary. We sometimes describe sufficiently advanced technology as indistinguishable from magic. But I think there's something even more profound about knowing, at least in broad strokes, how the magic works. Understanding the physics doesn't diminish the wonder. It amplifies it.

## A Civilization in Nineteen Feet

![](https://f005.backblazeb2.com/file/landons-blog/assets/posts/2026-10-13-spaceship-in-the-wilderness/IMG_2354.jpeg)

A few decades ago, replicating even a fraction of my present experience would have required an enormous amount of equipment, money, and logistical planning. Reliable communications from remote wilderness might have required specialized radio equipment or expensive satellite systems. Electricity would have depended on generators and the fuel necessary to operate them. Entertainment would have needed to be carried along in physical form. Access to information would have been limited to whatever books, maps, and reference materials one could bring. Working remotely in the manner I am now would have been practically impossible.

Today, I can bring nearly my entire professional and intellectual life into the mountains. I can participate in meetings, write software, read scientific papers, communicate with colleagues, access almost any published information, watch movies, and listen to music. I can do all of this while remaining almost completely independent of the physical infrastructure that ordinarily supports modern life. There's something remarkable about the scale of that independence.

My trailer is not particularly large or luxurious. It is a modest, nineteen-foot machine that I can hitch to the back of my pickup truck. Yet it contains an extraordinary concentration of humanity's accumulated technological achievements. It provides shelter, warmth, refrigeration, sanitation, electrical power, running water, and global communications.

For most of human history, possessing even some of these capabilities would have represented extraordinary wealth. Now they fit inside a vehicle small enough to park beside a forest road. There's something deeply satisfying about understanding the systems that make it all possible: solar panels generate electricity, batteries store it, propane fuels the furnace, and refrigeration preserves my food. As the days pass, my fresh water supply dwindles while the waste tanks gradually fill.

Nothing is infinite. Everything has a cost, a capacity, and a constraint. Living this way makes those relationships tangible. It's a little like operating a spacecraft, except my mission control consists mostly of checking the battery monitor and occasionally remembering to empty the black tank. Perhaps less glamorous than NASA, but the principles are surprisingly similar.

## The Disappearing Boundary

What I find most profound about this experience, however, isn't the technology itself, it's what the technology makes possible. For nearly all of human history, there has been a fundamental tension between participating in civilization and withdrawing into wilderness. Civilization offers security, community, information, opportunity, and access to the collective achievements of humanity. But those benefits have historically been tied to geography.

To participate deeply in civilization, one generally had to live where civilization was concentrated. Wilderness offered something different: solitude, independence, freedom of movement, and an encounter with the natural world largely undisturbed by human activity. But entering the wilderness meant leaving much of civilization behind. It meant surrendering conveniences, communication, access to information, and often the ability to earn a living. These were, for the most part, mutually exclusive ways of life.

For one of the first times in human history, that tradeoff is beginning to disappear. I can be physically isolated without being intellectually isolated. I can be geographically untethered without being professionally disconnected. I can withdraw into the mountains without withdrawing from the world. The place where I work no longer needs to be the place where I live. The place where I live no longer needs to be close to a city, an office, or even a cellular tower.

I can be surrounded by a landscape largely unchanged for centuries while remaining intimately connected to the most advanced technological systems humanity has ever constructed. That represents something more than convenience. It is a fundamentally different way of inhabiting the planet.

For generations, we organized our lives around the locations of economic opportunity. People left farms and rural communities for cities. Cities grew because the concentration of people, resources, and institutions made economic activity possible. Physical proximity was a prerequisite for participation. That relationship is changing. Not for everyone, and certainly not for every profession. The physical world still imposes constraints that no communications network can eliminate. Communities, relationships, and institutions still matter enormously. But for people whose work can be performed digitally, geography has become less of a master and more of a choice. There is something liberating about that.

The wilderness no longer has to be somewhere I visit before returning to my real life. For a little while, the wilderness can be where my real life happens.

## The Accumulated Genius of Humanity

I've also been thinking about how many people, across how many generations, were necessary to make this evening possible. The photovoltaic effect was first observed in the nineteenth century. Our understanding of electromagnetism emerged through decades of theoretical and experimental work. Semiconductors required discoveries in quantum mechanics, materials science, and manufacturing. Rocketry depended on advances in chemistry, mathematics, metallurgy, and propulsion.

Modern computing emerged from an enormous succession of innovations, each building upon the work of those who came before. No single person understands every system involved in my little campsite. No single person could build all of them. And yet, through the distributed intelligence and cooperation of millions of human beings, these systems exist, interoperate, and are accessible to ordinary individuals. 

This, to me, is one of the most beautiful things about civilization. We inherit the accumulated achievements of people we have never met, most of whom lived and died long before we were born. We build upon their discoveries, contribute our own small pieces, and pass the resulting knowledge onward.

In my own work, I spend my days building secure computing infrastructure for increasingly capable artificial intelligence. The systems I work on depend on decades of progress in operating systems, processors, virtualization, and computer security. And now I find myself using another product of that accumulated knowledge to work on those same systems from the side of a mountain. 

The whole arrangement feels wonderfully recursive. I am using the technological future to help build the technological future. And I get to do it from a place that feels almost timeless.

## A New Kind of Freedom

This past year has brought an enormous amount of change into my life. I've left behind a relationship, a home, and a city where I spent an enormous amount of my adulthood in. I've moved across the country and spent a great deal of time reconsidering what I want my life to look like.

Part of that process has involved spending more time outdoors. I've climbed mountains, wandered through forests, camped alone, watched storms roll across alpine valleys, and found myself increasingly drawn to the stillness of the natural world. There's something restorative about being reminded that the world is so much larger than the circumstances of one's own life.

Mountains do not care about careers, heartbreak, financial statements, or the thousands of little concerns that occupy our minds. They simply exist. And sometimes, being in their presence makes everything else feel a little smaller. But I'm also someone who genuinely loves technology. I love engineering. I love the astonishing things human beings are capable of creating. My curiosity about computers and science isn't something I wish to abandon when I escape into nature.

In the past, these two parts of my personality might have required a compromise. I could pursue a technically demanding career in a major city, or I could retreat into the wilderness in search of solitude. Now, to an extent that would have been difficult to imagine even ten years ago, I can do both. I can spend my evenings beneath the stars and my mornings working on some of the most advanced computing systems in existence. I can experience the silence of a forest and the intellectual excitement of participating in modern technological civilization. I don't have to choose one at the expense of the other. 

After a year in which so much of my life has felt beyond my control, there's something deeply meaningful about discovering this kind of freedom. Not freedom from responsibility, or from other people, or from the ordinary difficulties of being human, but freedom to decide where and how I want to live. Freedom to build a life that accommodates both my curiosity about the world and my desire to experience it. Freedom to make a home wherever I happen to find myself.

## The World Outside

It's getting late now.

Outside, the mountains are dark, and the temperature is dropping. I arrived after sunset, so I still haven't seen much of the landscape surrounding my campsite. I know there are aspens nearby, and mountains rising beyond them, but I'll have to wait until morning to see exactly where I've ended up.

Above me, hundreds of satellites are passing silently overhead. Tomorrow, the sun will rise over the mountains, and the solar panels on my roof will begin replenishing the energy I've consumed tonight.
I'll make coffee, open my laptop, and begin my workday.

At some point, I'll probably forget that there's anything particularly remarkable about what I'm doing. I'll get distracted by some frustrating technical problem, complain about a meeting, or become annoyed that the internet is running a little slower than usual. And that, perhaps, is what amazes me most, that something which would have seemed almost inconceivable just a few decades ago has become ordinary enough to complain about.

There's an entire wilderness beyond my campsite waiting to be seen. I think I'll log off tomorrow and go see it.
